A clinician discussion guide

When a patient
brings an AI answer.

“AI says I should have this test. Do I need it?” That question might come during an appointment, through the patient portal or by email. This guide covers the conversation itself, along with organizational policy, patient information, time and reimbursement.

Download the guide (PDF) →
Begin with the concern

What is the patient asking you to do?

A long answer may carry one concern. Start there before reviewing every claim.

Understand

“What prompted you to ask AI?”

Focus

“Which part concerns you most?”

Clarify

“Are you asking about the test, the diagnosis or the next step?”

Their questions can reveal what they understand, what they fear and what still needs explaining.
Decide where the work belongs

Not every request fits in a message.

Assess urgency first. Then choose an appropriate setting.

1. Answer through the portal

When the question can be addressed safely with the available information.

2. Arrange a visit or further review

When assessment, discussion or the amount of material requires more time.

3. Arrange more urgent assessment

When the patient’s symptoms or circumstances warrant prompt care.

Email: follow practice policy and direct clinical requests to the appropriate channel.

Put the answer in context

What did the AI know?

Compare the supplied information with the context needed for this decision.

What the patient gave the AI

Their question, symptoms or history, uploaded results and follow-up messages.

What else may matter clinically

Relevant history, medicines and timing, examination findings, and prior results and trends.

Look beyond the citations.

Do the studies apply here? How strong is the evidence? Does it support this conclusion for this patient?

Your own AI use and workplace policy

Reading it is different from uploading it.

Review the patient’s answer

Evaluate the relevant suggestion using clinical context and evidence.

Use your own AI tool?

Decide what it would add. You do not have to run the question through another model.

Before entering patient information

Check approved tools, accounts, tasks and data rules. Approval for documentation may not cover clinical decision support. Confirm documentation and disclosure requirements.

Two AI answers agreeing is not independent verification.
Explain the reasoning

Make the assessment understandable.

If it raises a useful possibility
“This is worth considering. Here is what would help us assess it.”
If you disagree
“Here is why I think this is less likely in your situation, and what I suggest we do next.”

Ask what remains unclear. Agree on the next step.

Time, payment and organizational support

This is clinical work.

Read, check, interpret, explain.

During a visit

Qualifying work may be reflected in E/M billing based on time or medical decision-making.

Through secure digital communication

Some work may qualify for online digital E/M billing (CPT 99421: 5–10 minutes; 99422: 11–20; 99423: 21 or more, cumulatively over seven days). Not every message does. Check payer and service requirements.

Billing eligibility is not the same as recognition of your time.

Practices need triage, scheduling, documentation and consent processes. Employed clinicians also need the work recognized in workload and compensation.

Sources and further reading

A proposed discussion guide to adapt to your practice. The message example is hypothetical. Billing notes are for the United States.
